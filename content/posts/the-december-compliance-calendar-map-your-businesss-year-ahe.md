Most business owners in Port Arthur can recite their best customer's last order from memory but not the date their liability policy renews. That is not a character flaw. Running a business fills your head with the things that matter day to day, and the paperwork dates that keep the business running get left to sticky notes and good intentions. The trouble is that a missed renewal, a lease that rolled over without a conversation, or a lapsed registration costs real money and real stress, usually in the middle of a busy work week.

December is the cure. Things tend to slow down around the holidays, and you are already thinking about the year ahead. A few focused hours now can turn a pile of scattered deadlines into one business compliance calendar that tells you what is due, what is renewing, and what deserves a second look before the clock starts.

## Why December is the right month to build a business compliance calendar

A compliance calendar is simply a single list of every date that can cost your business money or privileges if it slips. Insurance renewals, lease and contract terms, license and registration dates, payroll filings, and state and local filings all belong on it. The point is not to create more work. The point is to see the whole year at once, so you decide things on your schedule instead of reacting to somebody else's notice.

December works because it sits right before the busiest filing stretch. January brings payroll reports and a fourth-quarter estimated tax payment. Spring brings Texas franchise tax and property tax paperwork. If you map those now, nothing arrives as a surprise. The [SBA's guidance on managing your business](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business) makes the same basic point: owners who track their obligations in one place spend less time scrambling and more time on the work they trained for.

## Review insurance before the renewal notice does the talking

Renewal notices have a way of arriving with a new premium and a short fuse. If you review coverage 60 to 90 days ahead, you can ask questions and compare options while you still have leverage. Wait until the week of renewal and the easiest choice is to sign what is in front of you.

For most businesses, the review list looks like this:

- **Liability coverage.** Confirm what your general and professional liability policies actually cover, check the limits against your current revenue and activities, and ask what changes if you add services or locations.
- **Property and business interruption.** Port Arthur sits on the Gulf Coast, so windstorm and flood coverage deserve a direct question: if a storm closed your office for six weeks, would the policy cover payroll and rent during that time?
- **Cyber and general liability.** Customer and financial records are a target, and many policies now carry conditions about security practices. Read them before a claim, not after.
- **Equipment and key-person coverage.** Vehicles, machinery, and specialized tools are large assets. Make sure the schedule of equipment matches what is actually in the building.

While you are at it, ask your agent for quotes from at least two other carriers. Even if you stay put, a competing quote is a fair way to test the price. If you want a framework for deciding how much coverage is enough, our post on [how much insurance coverage you need](/resources/insuring-where-it-counts-avoid-over-insuring-in-the-wrong-pl) ties limits to net worth and cash flow instead of a generic policy checklist.

## Read the leases and contracts that renew on their own

Auto-renewal clauses are quiet. Nobody calls you to say your office lease will renew for another three years unless you send written notice 90 or 120 days before it ends. By the time you remember, the window has closed and the rent escalator has kicked in.

![Two professionals reviewing a lease agreement at a desk](/content-assets/reviewing-office-lease-agreement.jpg)

Say a three-owner company leases 4,200 square feet and the lease includes a 4 percent annual escalator with a 120-day notice requirement. Missing that window does not just lock in the rent. It removes your chance to ask for a lower escalator, a tenant improvement allowance, or a shorter term while you still have a reason to walk away. A calendar reminder 150 days out is cheap insurance.

Gather these documents in December and note the end date, notice deadline, and renewal terms for each:

- Office, retail, or warehouse lease
- Equipment leases and service agreements, such as vehicle, machinery, and copier contracts
- Billing company, software, and answering service agreements
- Staffing, contractor, and vendor agreements
- Software subscriptions that bill annually

## Collect license and credential dates in one place

Most business owners carry more renewal dates than they realize, and each one has its own agency, its own cycle, and its own consequences. A practical list looks like this:

- **Professional and trade licenses.** Note the renewal cycle and any continuing education requirements for you and your key staff. Know where you are in the cycle now, not in the last month.
- **State and local registrations.** Business registrations, sales tax permits, and local permits each run on their own schedule.
- **Industry certifications and permits.** Track anything your industry requires, along with how often it renews.
- **Vendor, customer, and payer approvals.** Approvals that need periodic revalidation can interrupt revenue if a request slips by.
- **Facility permits and inspections.** Each has its own date, and they rarely line up.

For the business side, the [SBA's licenses and permits page](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/apply-licenses-permits) is a handy starting point for what a business may need at the federal, state, and local levels. Confirm local requirements with the city or county, since those vary.

## Put state and federal filings on the same page

This is the part most owners hand to their CPA and then forget about, which is fine, as long as someone is tracking it. Here is a sample of what a business calendar typically carries.

| Item | Typical timing | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Payroll W-2s and Form 941 for Q4 | January 31 | Late filings bring penalties, and corrections are slow. |
| Fourth-quarter estimated tax payment | January 15 | Owners of pass-through businesses pay personally. |
| Texas franchise tax report and Public Information Report | May 15 | Required for most LLCs and corporations, even when no tax is due. |
| Business personal property rendition | April 15 | Filed with the county appraisal district for equipment and furniture. Confirm your local rules. |
| Retirement plan contributions and notices | Varies by plan | Deadlines depend on plan type and tax filing extensions. |
| Estimated tax payments | April 15, June 15, September 15 | Missing one can trigger underpayment penalties. |

A few notes on that table. Texas does not have a separate annual report for most entities in the way some states do. The franchise tax report and its Public Information Report do that job, and the Secretary of State and Comptroller can forfeit an entity's right to operate if reports go unfiled. Your payroll provider's year-end cutoff matters too, and [why that early December deadline works in your favor](/resources/why-your-payroll-companys-early-december-deadline-is-actuall) is worth five minutes before you reach for the holiday punch.

If you want to double-check federal dates, the [IRS small business and self-employed page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) lists current filing and payment requirements.

## Turn the list into a calendar your office will use

A list in a drawer does nothing. A calendar that sends reminders to the right person does. Here is a simple way to set it up in one afternoon.

1. **Pick one home.** A shared digital calendar that your office manager and your CPA can both see works better than a spreadsheet one person owns.
2. **Enter two dates for every item.** One is the deadline itself. The other is the decision date, which is the day you need to start reviewing, usually 60 to 120 days earlier.
3. **Assign an owner.** Every entry gets a name. If it belongs to everyone, it belongs to no one.
4. **Attach the paperwork.** Link the policy, the lease, or the registration confirmation right to the entry so no one hunts for it later.
5. **Schedule a quarterly check.** Fifteen minutes in March, June, September, and December keeps the calendar honest.

## Common Questions

**What should a business compliance calendar include?**

Start with insurance renewals, lease and contract end dates with their notice windows, license and registration dates, vendor and payer renewals, payroll filings, estimated tax payments, and the Texas franchise tax report. Add a decision date 60 to 120 days before each renewal, so you have time to compare options instead of simply accepting the terms.

**When should I review my business insurance before renewal?**

Begin 60 to 90 days ahead. That gives you time to request quotes from other carriers, ask about coverage gaps like windstorm, flood, or business interruption, and negotiate. Reviewing in the final week usually means accepting whatever the carrier offers, since there is no time left to compare anything.

**Do I need to file an annual state report in Texas?**

Most Texas entities do not file a standalone annual report. Instead, the franchise tax report and Public Information Report are due each May 15, and they are required for most LLCs and corporations even when no tax is owed. Missing them can put your entity's good standing at risk.
