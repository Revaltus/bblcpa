Every fall, owners of small practices in Port Arthur get the same nudge from a plan provider: send your safe harbor notice by December 1. So they scramble. Here is the part that rarely gets said out loud. If your plan uses a 3% nonelective contribution, there is no annual safe harbor notice to send at all. The deadline that matters moves somewhere else, and the design you pick decides which calendar you live on.

This article walks through the safe harbor 401(k) notice requirement, who it still applies to, how the 3% and 4% nonelective options work, and what each design costs on a realistic payroll. One caution up front: your plan document controls. Rules like these have details and exceptions, so confirm the specifics with your plan provider or third-party administrator before you act on anything here.

## Which safe harbor designs require a notice and which do not

A safe harbor 401(k) is a plan design that lets you skip the yearly ADP and ACP nondiscrimination tests, which are the tests that can force highly paid owners to take back contributions when staff participation runs low. The price of admission is a required employer contribution that vests immediately. There are two main ways to pay it:

- **A matching contribution.** The basic formula is 100% of the first 3% of pay an employee defers, plus 50% of the next 2%, for a maximum match of 4% of pay. An enhanced formula can be used if it is at least as generous at every deferral level.
- **A nonelective contribution.** The employer puts at least 3% of pay into the account of every eligible employee, whether or not that person defers a dollar.

Until plan years beginning in 2020, both designs came with the same annual notice requirement. The SECURE Act changed that for the nonelective version. A plan that uses the 3% nonelective contribution no longer has to send the annual safe harbor notice. A plan that uses a safe harbor match still does.

That split is the whole point of this article. The December 1 scramble is real for matching plans. For nonelective plans it is mostly a leftover habit.

## How the notice rule works for a matching plan

For a matching safe harbor plan, the [IRS notice requirement](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/notice-requirement-for-a-safe-harbor-401k-or-401m-plan) is a timing rule. Each eligible employee must receive the notice within a reasonable period before the plan year begins, and the IRS treats 30 to 90 days as reasonable. For a calendar-year plan, that window runs from about October 3 to December 2. Counting back 30 days from January 1 lands on December 2, and most providers say December 1 to leave a cushion.

Employees who become eligible after the window has opened get their notice on or before the day they become eligible. That matters for a dental office or veterinary clinic with turnover, because a new hire in March needs the notice too.

### What the notice has to say

The notice has to be written in plain language that an average employee can follow. It covers:

- The safe harbor match formula, and any other contributions the plan makes
- The plan, or plans, that receive the contributions
- The type and amount of pay that can be deferred
- How and when employees can make or change a deferral election
- Withdrawal and vesting rules
- How to get more information, such as the summary plan description

Most payroll and recordkeeping platforms generate this notice automatically. Your job is to confirm the formula in the notice matches the formula in your plan document, word for word in effect. A notice that promises a 4% match when the document says something else is its own headache.

### What happens if it is late

A missed or late notice on a matching plan means the plan can lose its safe harbor status for that year. The plan then has to pass the ADP and ACP tests like any other 401(k). If the owner, who is almost always a highly compensated employee, defers the maximum while the staff defer little, the test can fail and the owner gets excess contributions returned as taxable income. There are correction routes, but they cost time and sometimes money. Sending the notice on time is far cheaper.

## Why the nonelective design changes the timing math

With a 3% nonelective contribution, the notice requirement is gone, and the amendment timing becomes far more forgiving than the old rules. Under the SECURE Act, a plan that did not previously have a safe harbor design can adopt a nonelective safe harbor in the middle of the year, as long as the amendment is made at least 30 days before the plan year ends. For a calendar-year plan, that is roughly December 1. Funny how that date keeps showing up.

### The 3% route

If you amend by that deadline and contribute at least 3% of pay to every eligible employee, the plan is a safe harbor plan for the whole year. The contribution is calculated on the full year's pay, not just pay after the amendment. So a December 1 decision still costs 3% of twelve months of payroll.

That can be a useful tool. A physician group that reaches November and sees the testing results heading the wrong way has an exit: adopt the 3% nonelective contribution and the tests no longer apply for that year. But it works only if you are willing to fund the full amount.

### The 4% fallback and what it costs

Miss the 30-day cutoff and there is a second window, at a higher price. A plan can still be amended to a nonelective safe harbor after that point if the contribution is at least 4% of pay, and the amendment is made by the end of the following plan year. For a calendar-year plan that means as late as December 31 of the next year.

That extra percentage point is the cost of waiting. On a $580,000 eligible payroll, 3% is $17,400 and 4% is $23,200. The difference is $5,800 for the same plan, the same employees, and a later signature.

## Match versus nonelective, with real numbers

Choosing a design is a math problem, and the answer depends on who actually defers. Take a Southeast Texas dental practice with an owner dentist paid $250,000 in W-2 wages and six staff members paid $330,000 combined. Total eligible pay is $580,000. The table assumes the basic match formula and that everyone who defers puts in at least 5% of pay, which earns the full 4% match.

| Who defers | Pay of participants | Basic safe harbor match (4%) | 3% nonelective |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owner and two staff | $350,000 | $14,000 | $17,400 |
| Owner and three staff | $415,000 | $16,600 | $17,400 |
| Everyone | $580,000 | $23,200 | $17,400 |

The pattern is worth studying. A match is cheaper when participation is low and more expensive when it is high. A nonelective contribution costs the same either way, which makes it easier to budget. It also reaches employees who cannot afford to defer, which tends to help with retention in a practice where front-desk and assistant roles are hard to fill.

There is a flip side. If only the owner and one or two employees defer, the match costs the least. And a nonelective contribution is a real expense on every paycheck, even for employees who never touch the plan.

### Other things the nonelective design affects

- **Predictability.** The cost is 3% of eligible pay. Your payroll system can calculate it, and your cash flow forecast can include it.
- **Admin load.** No annual safe harbor notice means one less item on the fall calendar, though you still owe employees the standard plan information.
- **Top-heavy rules.** A safe harbor 401(k) that holds only deferrals and safe harbor contributions is generally exempt from the top-heavy minimum, which is one more thing off the testing list.
- **Deduction.** Employer contributions are generally deductible for the business, so the after-tax cost is lower than the table suggests.

Those last two points are why owners who feel the plan is mostly for themselves still end up choosing a nonelective design. Your payroll folks can run the numbers for you. ## Timing cheat sheet for a calendar-year plan

Putting the three designs side by side makes the difference clear. This assumes an existing plan with a January 1 to December 31 plan year, and the dates are approximate. Your plan document and provider set the exact ones.

| Design | Notice to employees | Amendment timing | Can you decide late? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Safe harbor match | Required, 30 to 90 days before the plan year (about Oct. 3 to Dec. 2) | Before the plan year starts | No. Match plans cannot be adopted retroactively |
| 3% nonelective | No annual safe harbor notice | At least 30 days before year end (about Dec. 1) | Yes, until about December 1 of the plan year |
| 4% nonelective | No annual safe harbor notice | By the end of the following plan year | Yes, up to a full year later, at a higher cost |

The [IRS retirement plans page](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans) is the best starting point for the official rules and plan-type guidance if you want to read them yourself.

## Design traps that still apply to nonelective plans

Dropping the notice does not make a nonelective plan hands-off. A few conditions still matter.

**Every eligible employee gets the contribution.** That includes people who defer nothing, and the contribution cannot be tied to working the last day of the year or logging a minimum number of hours. The plan can still define who is eligible, such as age and service rules, but once someone is eligible they are in.

**The money vests immediately.** Safe harbor contributions are 100% vested from day one. One exception is a qualified automatic contribution arrangement, or QACA, which can use a two-year vesting schedule but comes with its own automatic enrollment requirements and a separate notice.

**Employees still need a real chance to defer.** Even without the safe harbor notice, your staff should have a reasonable window to start or change their deferrals, and they should get the plan's standard disclosures. A plan provider will usually handle this through the enrollment package and the summary plan description.

**The plan document has to say what you intend.** A plan written for a matching formula and run as a nonelective plan is a mismatch, and mismatches are exactly what auditors look for. If you are changing designs for next year, the amendment is the step that makes it official.

**New plans have their own timing.** A brand-new 401(k) can generally adopt safe harbor status after the plan year starts if enough of the year remains, and rules for a plan's first year differ from the ones above. Ask your provider before assuming.

## How to choose your design before the calendar chooses for you

The owners who get squeezed in late November are the ones who have not compared the options. The ones who sleep fine in December usually did four things in the summer.

1. **Pull the participation numbers.** Which employees defer now, at what rates, and how has that changed over three years? A practice where 70% of staff defer will see a match cost more than 3%.
2. **Price both designs on actual payroll.** Use the same method as the table above: run the match cost under low, expected, and high participation, then compare it to 3% of total eligible pay.
3. **Decide what the plan is for.** If the goal is keeping the owner's own deferral limit open without refunds, the nonelective design is often the simplest route. If the goal is rewarding people who save, a match fits better.
4. **Set the date.** Whatever you choose, put the amendment deadline and any notice deadline on the calendar before October.

## Common Questions

**Do I have to send a safe harbor 401(k) notice if I use the 3% nonelective contribution?**

No. For plan years beginning after 2019, the SECURE Act eliminated the annual safe harbor notice for nonelective plans. You still need a plan document that reflects the 3% contribution and should give employees the usual plan information and a reasonable chance to defer. Matching safe harbor plans still must send the notice.

**What is the safe harbor notice deadline for a calendar-year plan?**

For a matching plan, the notice must go out between 30 and 90 days before January 1, which is about October 3 to December 2. Most providers use December 1 as the working deadline to build in a cushion. New hires who become eligible later get the notice on or before their eligibility date.

**Can I switch to safe harbor in the middle of the year?**

Only with a nonelective contribution. A plan can generally adopt a 3% nonelective safe harbor if it is amended at least 30 days before year end, about December 1 for a calendar-year plan. After that, a 4% nonelective is allowed if the amendment is made by the end of the following plan year. Match designs cannot be adopted retroactively.

**Is a safe harbor match or a nonelective contribution cheaper?**

It depends on participation. A basic match costs up to 4% of pay for employees who defer, so it is cheaper when few people participate and costlier when most do. A nonelective contribution costs 3% of pay for every eligible employee regardless. Modeling both on your actual payroll is the only reliable way to compare them.
