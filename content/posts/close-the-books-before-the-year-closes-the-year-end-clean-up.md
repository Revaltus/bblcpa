*By Brammer, Begnaud & Lattimore, CPAs — Accounting, tax and advisory firm in Port Arthur, serving clients in Jefferson County and throughout Southeast Texas.*

Most business owners we meet in Southeast Texas are not behind on their taxes. They are behind on their books. The business checking account has not been reconciled since August, the credit card holds a mix of office supplies and a weekend in Galveston, and a $14,000 piece of equipment is sitting in "Supplies." None of that is a crisis in October. By the second week of January, it is a scramble, and somebody has to pay for the untangling.

The cheapest fix is a Q4 clean-up. Reconcile every account, separate personal spending from business spending, and correct miscategorized transactions before December 31. This post is a year-end bookkeeping checklist for taxes, written for business owners who would rather spend January running the business than chasing bank statements.

## Why messy books raise your tax prep bill

A tax preparer cannot file from a pile of unresolved questions. Every unexplained deposit, unlabeled charge, and account that does not tie to its statement turns into an email, a phone call, or an hour of billable cleanup. Under hourly billing, that time lands on your invoice.

Here is an illustrative example. Say a small business runs about 1,200 transactions a year. If 5 percent are miscoded, that is 60 entries someone has to find, question, and fix. At an assumed $200 an hour, five extra hours of cleanup adds $1,000 to the return before anyone has looked for a single deduction.

The cost is not only fees. Miscoded books hide the deductions you earned and can inflate the income you report. The [IRS recordkeeping guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/recordkeeping) is clear that your records need to support every line on the return. Clean books are how you meet that standard without a fire drill.

## Reconcile every account before December 31

Reconciliation means matching what your books say to what the bank, card issuer, or processor says. If the two do not agree, something is missing, duplicated, or wrong. Do it for every account, not only the main one.

For most businesses, the list looks like this:

- **Operating and payroll checking accounts.** Match each month's ending balance to the statement. Investigate any difference, even a small one.
- **Credit cards.** Business cards, and any personal card that touched business expenses. Make sure card payments are not counted twice, once as a payment and once as the underlying charges.
- **Customer and payer deposits.** Some customers and payers send one lump deposit covering many invoices. Tie each deposit to its remittance details so revenue is recorded at the gross amount, not the net.
- **Card processor deposits.** Customer payments made by card arrive net of processing fees. Those fees are an expense. If your books only show the net deposit, you are understating both revenue and expenses.
- **Loans and equipment financing.** Principal is not an expense. Interest is. Confirm the loan balance on your books matches the lender's year-end statement.
- **Payroll liability accounts.** Withholdings and payroll taxes should clear to zero after each filing. A lingering balance usually means something was posted incorrectly.

Do not wait for December 31 to start. Aim to have November reconciled by mid-December, then reconcile December as soon as the final statements post. If you use payroll, mind the cutoff too. Your payroll company's early-December deadline [exists to protect your W-2s](/resources/why-your-payroll-companys-early-december-deadline-is-actuall), and corrections are far easier before the year closes.

## Separate personal spending from business spending

Commingling is one of the most common problems we find in small business books, and it is easy to fall into. You buy shop supplies and groceries in the same trip. You pay a software subscription from a personal card because it was the one in your wallet. The business account covers a family dinner.

Three reasons to clean this up before year-end:

1. **Deductions get disputed.** If the IRS asks about a charge, you need to show it was a business expense. A mixed account makes that harder.
2. **Liability protection can weaken.** If your business is an LLC or other entity, paying personal bills from the entity account blurs the line that entity is supposed to draw.
3. **Your numbers lie.** A business that looks less profitable than it is leads to wrong decisions about hiring, equipment, and retirement contributions.

The fix is not complicated. Go through the year and tag each personal charge in the business accounts as an owner draw (or a distribution, depending on your entity type). Tag any business expense you paid personally as a reimbursable expense. Then, from January 1, run business spending through business accounts only. One business card used for business expenses, and one personal card for everything else, solves most of it.

The [IRS small business resources](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) are a good place to check how the agency treats business versus personal expenses.

## Fix miscategorized transactions while they are still easy to find

Categorization errors rarely look dramatic. They look like a line in "Office Expense" that should have been "Supplies," or a deposit labeled "Income" that was actually a customer refund reversal. But categories feed the tax return, and some errors change your tax bill.

These are the ones that show up most often in small business books:

- **Equipment coded as supplies.** An $18,000 piece of equipment is not a supply. It is a fixed asset that may qualify for depreciation or expensing, and the choice affects your tax bill for the year. Coding it wrong can mean missed or misapplied deductions.
- **Loan principal coded as expense.** This understates profit and can cause a mismatch with the lender's year-end statement.
- **Owner draws coded as salary or expenses.** This distorts payroll reports and profit.
- **Licenses, insurance, and training all lumped into "Professional Fees."** These are generally deductible, but separating them makes the return easier to prepare and easier to defend.
- **Business travel with personal days mixed in.** A conference in Austin with a few vacation days attached needs the business portion identified.
- **Customer refunds coded as expenses.** Refunds reduce revenue. Coding them as expenses overstates both sides.
- **1099 contractors without a tag.** If you pay a freelancer, a billing service, or a consultant $600 or more, you may owe a 1099-NEC by January 31. Tag those vendors now and collect their W-9s before they become hard to reach.

A good way to catch these is to run a transaction detail report for your largest categories and scan for items that do not belong. Then look at "Uncategorized" and "Ask My Accountant" accounts. Those should be empty by year-end.

## A year-end bookkeeping checklist for business owners

This table puts the work in order. The dates are targets, not IRS deadlines, built to leave room before the January rush.

| Task | What to check | Target date |
|---|---|---|
| Reconcile bank accounts | Every account ties to its December statement | Early January, with November done by Dec 15 |
| Reconcile credit cards | No duplicate payments, no personal charges left in | Early January |
| Tie out customer and processor deposits | Gross revenue matches remittances, fees recorded as expense | Dec 20 |
| Separate personal spending | Personal charges moved to owner draws | Dec 20 |
| Review large categories | Equipment, loans, refunds, and training coded correctly | Dec 28 |
| Clear uncategorized items | Suspense accounts at zero | Dec 31 |
| Collect W-9s and confirm 1099 vendors | Anyone paid $600 or more is tagged | Dec 31 |
| Confirm payroll is final | Last payroll posted, tax deposits matched | Per payroll company cutoff |
| Gather loan and asset documents | Year-end loan statements, equipment invoices | Jan 5 |

## Common Questions

**How early should I start year-end bookkeeping cleanup?**

Start in October. That gives you time to reconcile accounts, chase missing statements, and fix coding while the year is still open. Reconcile November by mid-December. Anything you find after December 31 can still be corrected on the books, but moves like equipment purchases or retirement contributions are harder to adjust.

**Can I deduct expenses I paid from a personal account?**

Often, yes, if the expense was ordinary and necessary for your business and you can document it. Record it as a reimbursable expense or an owner contribution, depending on your entity type, and keep the receipt. The deduction is not lost by using the wrong card, but the paper trail gets harder to defend.

**Will cleaner books really lower my tax prep fees?**

Usually, yes. If your preparer bills hourly, reconciled accounts, coded transactions, and collected W-9s remove the hours spent chasing answers. If you are on a fixed fee, clean books mean fewer follow-up questions, a faster filing, and more time for planning that actually reduces your tax bill.
