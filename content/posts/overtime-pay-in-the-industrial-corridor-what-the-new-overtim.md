Turnaround season in Port Arthur produces some of the biggest paychecks of the year. A pipefitter working 12-hour days for six weeks watches overtime stack up fast, and the new federal overtime deduction sounds like it should make all of that money tax free. It does not. Only the premium portion of overtime qualifies, and whether a worker can claim it cleanly depends a lot on how the employer coded the pay.

This post walks through the rules as the [IRS describes them](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/one-big-beautiful-bill-act-tax-deductions-for-working-americans-and-seniors), with Southeast Texas numbers, for the workers who earn the overtime and the employers who run payroll. Tax guidance on these deductions is still being refined, so treat the details below as a snapshot and confirm anything that affects your return before you file.

## Why a turnaround paycheck does not become tax free

The deduction is for "qualified overtime compensation," and it runs for tax years 2025 through 2028. Three points trip people up.

First, it is a deduction, not an exclusion. Your overtime still shows up in your wages, and the deduction reduces your taxable income on your Form 1040. You can claim it whether you itemize or take the standard deduction.

Second, it only covers the overtime your employer is required to pay under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act. That means hours over 40 in a workweek, paid at one and a half times your regular rate. Of that pay, only the extra half counts. The "straight time" portion of an overtime hour is ordinary wages.

Third, payroll taxes do not change. Social Security and Medicare tax still apply to every dollar of overtime. The deduction affects federal income tax only. Texas has no state income tax, so for Port Arthur households the federal return is the whole story.

## A worked example with real turnaround numbers

Take a pipefitter earning $32 an hour. During a six-week turnaround, he works 12-hour days, six days a week. That is 72 hours a week, or 32 overtime hours each week, for 192 overtime hours total.

| Pay component | Hours | Rate | Amount | Qualifies for deduction? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Regular hours | 240 | $32 | $7,680 | No |
| Straight-time portion of overtime | 192 | $32 | $6,144 | No |
| Overtime premium (the extra half) | 192 | $16 | $3,072 | Yes |
| Total pay | | | $16,896 | $3,072 deductible |

At time and a half, the premium is always one-third of the overtime pay. His overtime paid $9,216, and $3,072 of it qualifies. If he sits in the 22 percent federal bracket, that deduction saves him roughly $676 in income tax. Real money, but a long way from "no tax on overtime."

Now add a wrinkle common on industrial job sites. Suppose the union agreement or contractor policy pays double time on Sundays. At $32 an hour, that is $64. Only the FLSA-required premium of $16 qualifies. The other $16 per hour is a contractual bonus the law does not require, so it stays out of the deduction. The same goes for daily overtime after eight hours when the week stays under 40.

Salaried employees who are exempt from overtime rules, such as many supervisors, do not have qualified overtime at all, however many hours they work.

## Limits and income phase-outs that trim the deduction

The deduction has a ceiling and an income test.

- **Annual cap:** up to $12,500 per return, or $25,000 for joint filers.
- **Phase-out:** the cap shrinks by $100 for every $1,000 of modified adjusted gross income above $150,000 ($300,000 for joint filers). A single filer at $200,000 sees the cap fall from $12,500 to $7,500.
- **Valid Social Security number:** required, and married couples must file jointly to claim it.

Most hourly industrial workers will not hit the cap. At a $16 premium, you would need about 780 overtime hours in a year to reach $12,500. The phase-out matters more for two-income households where one spouse has a high salary, say a physician married to a plant operator who works heavy overtime. In that case the household income, not the overtime itself, decides how much of the deduction survives.

## Tips follow similar rules with a different cap

Port Arthur's restaurants, hotels, and casino-adjacent hospitality jobs generate tipped income, and the companion deduction for qualified tips works on a similar model.

- It runs for tax years 2025 through 2028.
- The cap is $25,000 per return.
- The same income phase-out applies, starting at $150,000 ($300,000 joint).
- Tips must be voluntary, not a negotiated or mandatory charge.
- The worker's occupation must be one that customarily and regularly received tips, based on a list from the Treasury Department.

Tips remain subject to Social Security and Medicare tax, and they must still be reported to the employer. That is where employer payroll reporting becomes the deciding factor. A server who reports cash tips accurately and whose credit card tips flow through payroll has a paper trail. A server whose tips were handled informally has a harder time proving what the deduction should be.

## Payroll records that make claiming easier

For 2025, the IRS gave employers transition relief, so the new deductions did not require a brand-new W-2 reporting format right away. The IRS has signaled that more detailed reporting is coming, so employers should assume that separate tracking will matter going forward. Check the final W-2 instructions each year rather than assuming last year's setup still works.

Meanwhile, employees still need numbers to put on a return. Here is what a clean payroll file looks like.

1. **Separate pay codes.** Regular wages, FLSA overtime, and contractual extras like double time or shift bonuses should each have their own code. Mixing them forces guesswork later.
2. **Overtime premium tracked or easily calculated.** If the system records overtime pay at time and a half, the premium is one-third of it. If it records anything different, document the method.
3. **Tips by type.** Cash tips reported by employees, credit card tips, and any tip pooling should be distinguishable in payroll.
4. **Occupation on file.** Tip eligibility depends on the job, so a clear job title for each tipped employee helps.
5. **Correct Social Security numbers.** A mismatch can hold up an otherwise valid claim.
6. **Exempt versus nonexempt status.** Misclassified employees create both wage-law and tax-reporting problems.

The [IRS employment tax page for small businesses](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/employment-taxes) is the best starting point for the filing side. Small employers beyond the refineries feel this too. A veterinary clinic paying vet techs overtime during a busy season, or a dental office with hourly hygienists and assistants, has the same recordkeeping questions as a contractor with 40 pipefitters.

## What workers should gather before filing

If you earn overtime or tips, pull these together before your appointment or your software session:

- Your final pay stub of the year, which often shows year-to-date overtime hours and pay.
- Your W-2, plus any statement your employer provides breaking out overtime or tips.
- Your contract or pay policy showing how overtime is calculated.
- Records of any cash tips you reported to your employer.
- A valid Social Security number for you and your spouse.

## Common Questions

**Is overtime pay now tax free?**

No. The deduction applies only to the premium portion, the extra half of time and a half, and only for federal income tax. Social Security and Medicare taxes still apply. The maximum is $12,500 per return ($25,000 joint), reduced for modified adjusted gross income above $150,000 ($300,000 joint), for tax years 2025 through 2028.

**Does the overtime deduction lower my Texas state taxes?**

Texas has no personal income tax, so there is no state return to benefit. The savings come entirely from your federal return. Your employer's payroll taxes, including Social Security and Medicare, are unchanged, so your take-home pay per check does not automatically rise unless you adjust your withholding.

**What if my employer's W-2 does not show my overtime premium?**

Start with your final pay stub and your employer's payroll records, which usually show overtime hours and pay. The IRS gave employers transition relief for 2025, so separate W-2 reporting was not always required. A CPA can calculate the premium from those records and document the method in case questions come up later.
